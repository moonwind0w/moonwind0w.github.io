I’m deeply fascinated by digital ephemera and the strange kind of immortality that occurs when we upload pieces of ourselves online. Digital technology has transformed how we collect, preserve, and revisit memories. Today, fragments of our lives drift into the forgotten corners of the internet, waiting to be rediscovered by strangers years later, or perhaps never seen again. Yet, nothing online is guaranteed, which is why digital preservation and archivists are so crucial to our cultural heritage.

These sentiments inform my goals and artistic purpose as a creative, and I hope to invoke both the familiar and the unknown through my work. In archivist fashion, my multimedia methods also come from a place of preservation. I seek to bring new life to mediums that are both forgotten and underutilized, and to share with the world the beauty in the mundane and the ordinary. I want to leave you with a short piece I wrote on Digital Memory:

Digital Memory

“Your house is like a museum of everything you have ever loved,” I say to my mother as we sift through someone’s once-cherished collection of VHS tapes at an estate sale. Around me are thick scrapbooks with handwritten notes, framed family portraits, film negatives, and stacks of physical media. Each object feels like a clue into someone’s life—an archive curated unintentionally through years of living.

My iPhone buzzes, and as I stand in what feels like a time capsule, I am struck by a realization: so much of my everyday life—my photos, messages, journals, playlists, search histories, and most of my personal data—exists within this single device. Standing in a room filled with physical memories, I begin to wonder: If so much of my life is born digitally, how will I be remembered? More importantly, how will we be remembered? The questions overwhelm me. What deserves to be preserved? Whose responsibility is it? I laugh to myself and think, “Will my grandchildren be able to see my Snapchat memories?”

Digital convergence has enabled us to capture and record our lives with unprecedented ease and volume, yet this abundance presents numerous challenges. Our digital footprints are fragile, and something being online does not mean it is safe from loss. In a December 2025 BBC article titled Why Your Early 2000s Photos Are Probably Lost Forever, Julia Bensfield Luce (2025) focuses on personal photography and the risks of digital loss.

If you used a digital camera in the early 2000s, there’s a good chance whole chapters of your life have been erased. A generation of photos has vanished on broken hard drives and defunct websites. (Luce, 2025)

Luce (2025) highlights how early digital storage methods and social media platforms resulted in obsolete and inaccessible images. The massive amount of digital data we produce makes it harder to decide what should be preserved—not only for personal archives but for collective memory. Digital technology allows us to record our lives with exceptional ease, but its impermanence and reliance on unstable platforms threaten to erase entire chapters of personal and cultural history.

At some point, most of us have heard the phrase, “Anything you post on the internet will last forever!” While monitoring what you share online is wise, the truth is that the internet is not forever. Luce (2025) discusses how MySpace lost 12 years’ worth of data in a server crash, erasing years of personal and cultural history.

Experts recommend redundancy in preservation practices. The Photo Managers promote the “3-2-1 rule” of photography storage: three copies of every photo, stored on at least two different media types, with one copy kept in a separate physical location (as cited in Luce, 2025). If consumers rely solely on cloud storage, their memories remain dependent on corporations that ultimately control access and survival.

According to Woolf (2023), smartphones capture 92.5% of all pictures, leaving just 7.5% to conventional cameras. With cameras embedded in devices we carry constantly, the output of personal photography is higher than ever. Without intentional curation and preservation, much of this digital record may disappear.

In a society dependent on digital technology, how will future historians understand the intricacies of our digital lives if those lives cannot be accessed? Photographs serve as portals into the past. The museum of our lives now exists within our iPhones, but we retain the power to preserve it: if we take the necessary steps.
